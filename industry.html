FOLLOW THE VALUE CHAIN

AI is a stack. Nobody builds it alone.

01 / COMPUTE

Start with the physical world

Chip equipment enables fabrication. Foundries manufacture chips. Designers turn those chips into accelerators for AI workloads.

02 / INTELLIGENCE

Compute becomes capability

Cloud providers operate compute infrastructure. Model labs use it to train and serve systems that generate text, images, and code.

03 / PRODUCTS

Capability finds a customer

Data platforms and applications connect models to useful work. Money, distribution, and feedback flow back through the stack.

FROM THE REEL · THE “AI BUBBLE” MAP

The loop, step by step.

Money goes in as investment, comes back as compute revenue, and the same companies sell the models. Customers, investors, distributors and competitors — all at once. Map total shown on screen: $26 trillion.

  1. Investors fund the labs — Microsoft holds roughly 27% of OpenAI; NVIDIA puts $30 billion into OpenAI; SoftBank, OpenAI’s biggest outside investor, builds the data centre OpenAI leases through SB Energy; Amazon and Microsoft invest in Anthropic; AMD agrees up to $5 billion into Anthropic; NVIDIA invests in Anthropic; Google holds a large Anthropic stake, keeps investing, and backs $5 billion of loans Anthropic uses to lease data centres.
  2. Labs spend it back on compute — OpenAI buys NVIDIA chips in bulk, rents Oracle capacity, and uses Microsoft, Amazon and Google clouds plus CoreWeave; Anthropic runs on Google Cloud and commits $100 billion across Microsoft and Amazon clouds combined (split not stated).
  3. Suppliers book it and reinvest — Oracle buys tens of billions in NVIDIA chips for its data centres; NVIDIA invests in CoreWeave and $5 billion in Intel, buys CoreWeave’s unused capacity, and guarantees OpenAI’s SB Energy lease; CoreWeave stocks its centres with NVIDIA GPUs; OpenAI holds $350 million of CoreWeave stock.
  4. The same companies distribute the models — the clouds sell compute to the labs and sell the labs’ models onward, while building rival models of their own; Google sells compute to OpenAI while holding Anthropic; Meta buys capacity from Google Cloud and CoreWeave while running its own AI labs.
  5. Everyone competes while cooperating — that is the reel’s punchline: a customer is also a distribution partner, an investor, and a competitor.
  6. The reel’s question — the revenue is real, but is it enough to cover hundreds of billions in future commitments? If one company fails, the shock runs through the whole loop.

FROM THE NEWS · MARVELL–LUMENTUM

Marvell buys Lumentum.

Marvell Technology is buying optical-components maker Lumentum in an all-stock deal. Terms as stated in the segment: 4.5 Marvell shares per Lumentum share, about $1,132 per share, a 23.9% premium to the last price before talks were rumoured on 28 October. Shares that morning, as stated: Marvell up about 3.3%, Lumentum up 2.5%.

Why, as framed in the segment: Marvell is betting big-tech AI spending stays high, keeping demand up for the optical components that move data at high speeds. The post’s written caption adds two things the spoken segment did not: a $115 billion deal value, and the framing that this steps up Marvell’s challenge to Broadcom.

Also from the segment, citing reports: NVIDIA invested $2 billion in each company earlier in 2026 — no further detail given. No closing date or completion terms were stated. The exact cents on the per-share price were unclear, so this section says about $1,132.

What changes on the map: two new silicon-layer players (Marvell, Lumentum) and three new dashed lines — the acquisition plus the two reported NVIDIA investments. The two new profiles are reel-sourced, not primary-product-reviewed; they are the exception to the notice at the top of the page.

Update, 2 Nov: the announced terms are corroborated — $115B is the implied enterprise value including debt; 4.5 Marvell shares per Lumentum share (~$1,132 at 28 Oct prices, 23.9% premium); expected close H1 2028 subject to shareholder and regulatory approval. NVIDIA's $2B investments in each were made in March 2026. The dollar deal value will move with Marvell's share price until close because the ratio is fixed.

SILICON · THE AI SUPPLY CHAIN

Who supplies the AI build-out.

NVIDIA gets the headlines, but the build-out runs on a chain: tools → wafers → accelerators → memory → links. Four players join the map here — Intel, SK Hynix, Micron, Samsung — completing the silicon layer at 11 players.

  1. Tools — ASML. The only company that builds EUV lithography machines. Every leading-edge chip passes through them; no ASML, no next node.
  2. Wafers — TSMC. Fabricates NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom and Apple chips. Leading-edge capacity is scarce, and scarcity is pricing power.
  3. Accelerators — NVIDIA (platform plus CUDA moat), AMD (the alternative, gaining), Broadcom (custom silicon for hyperscalers), Marvell (custom silicon plus optical links), Intel (CPUs plus Gaudi, and a foundry bet).
  4. Memory — SK Hynix, Samsung, Micron. Only three companies make HBM, the stacked memory every accelerator needs to feed its compute. HBM is the binding constraint: accelerators cannot ship without stacks of it, and capacity takes 12–18 months to expand.
  5. Links — Lumentum (optical components moving data between accelerators), plus Broadcom and Marvell networking. When compute scales out, moving data becomes the bottleneck after making it.

Winners and losers below are an editorial read from supply-chain position and the earnings in the post — not a verified market ranking.

  • Winning so far: NVIDIA. The platform and the CUDA moat. Everyone else's roadmap is a reaction to it.
  • Winning so far: TSMC and ASML. TSMC makes everyone's chips; ASML makes the machines that make the chips. Both sell scarcity.
  • Winning so far: SK Hynix. First to qualify HBM with NVIDIA, and the largest HBM share (about half of HBM revenue in Q2 2026, per Counterpoint Research as cited in HBM tracking).
  • Winning so far: Broadcom. Custom silicon for hyperscalers plus AI networking — the quiet compounder of the stack.
  • Winning, not leading: AMD. The post's evidence: Q3 beat on revenue ($13.38B vs $13.0B expected), adjusted EPS ($2.09 vs $1.97), and data-centre revenue up 97%. The MI line is gaining share, and AMD is now chasing memory supply directly (a multi-year Samsung agreement). Winning share is not winning the race — NVIDIA still sets the pace.
  • Catching up: Samsung and Micron. Samsung has memory scale, HBM4 qualification, and a foundry that still trails TSMC. Micron's 2026 HBM output is reported sold out, but its share is smaller than SK Hynix's. Both are levered to the same shortage.
  • Levered to the build-out: Marvell and Lumentum. Custom silicon and optical links ride AI connectivity demand; their deal still has to close (expected H1 2028).
  • Losing so far: Intel. The post's evidence: Q3 beat expectations, but guidance came in well below analysts' projections. It missed the accelerator wave, and its foundry turnaround is unproven — 14A is the test, with risk production in 2027. The bull case now rests on execution (first high-volume High-NA logic product shipped July 2026), NVIDIA's $5 billion investment (drawn as an edge above), and the US government's 10% stake (text only, not a map player). Losing is not out — but the comeback is a multi-year call, not a quarter.

On the AMD-versus-Intel question directly: your read matches the evidence so far. AMD's data-centre business is growing fast; Intel's guidance disappointed. The nuance is that AMD is winning share while NVIDIA wins the race, and Intel's foundry bet is the one part of this map where the verdict is still years away.

The four new profiles are general-knowledge additions, primary product reference not yet reviewed — the same exception as Marvell and Lumentum. One slide in the carousel prints a Q4 outlook figure that does not fit a roughly $13B quarterly run-rate, so this section does not use it.

APPS · AI CODING TOOLS

The coding-agent land grab.

Two deals in one post, pointing the same way: the coding-agent race is moving from the model to the workflow around it. Two new players join the map here — Conductor and Pierre Computer — taking it to 53 players.

  • Meta acquires Conductor. Founded in 2024 by Charlie Holtz and Jackson de Campos, Conductor lets developers assign coding tasks to AI agents, track their progress, and review their work in one place. The post's read: it helps Meta expand its AI coding tools and attract more developers to its ecosystem. The deeper read: coding agents are going from one agent per task to fleets of agents, and fleets need orchestration — Conductor is that orchestration layer.
  • OpenAI plans to acquire Pierre Computer. A startup focused on code storage and management. If Conductor is the fleet manager, Pierre is the warehouse: agents that write code at machine speed need somewhere to keep and manage it. Note the tense — the post says OpenAI has announced plans, so this is not a closed deal, and it is drawn as a planned acquisition.
  • The pattern. Models are converging; the workflow around them — orchestration, storage, review — is where differentiation moves. That is also the stack this site's own learning threads keep returning to: running agents, harnesses, and the human review step. The frontier labs are now buying the workflow, not just building the model.

What the post does not state: no price, no closing date, and no terms for either deal — so none are shown here. Both new profiles are post-sourced additions, primary product reference not yet reviewed — the same exception as the recent silicon additions. The post cites Bloomberg as its source.

Source: The Rundown AI post, 4 Nov 2026. As stated in the post, paraphrased, not verified.
